  Part 1. The thesis, stated honestly

  1.1 Why the two halves belong together
  When a lender rejects an applicant, two questions follow.

       Part A, diagnostic attribution: why was I rejected? The lender must give specific
       principal reasons.
       Part B, prescriptive recourse: what can I change to be approved next time? When the
       person returns after Δt, the retrained model M_(t+Δt) decides.

  What the law says (               ⚠️ confirm current text before quoting it in a paper):
       US ECOA / Regulation B requires specific principal reasons for adverse action. The
       official commentary treats about four reasons as sufficient. CFPB Circular 2022-03
       says that using a complex algorithm does not excuse the duty to give specific reasons.
       US FCRA §609(f) requires the key factors that adversely affected a credit score: at
       most four, plus credit inquiries if they were a key factor.
       Neither statute requires a recourse plan. Recourse is a best-practice and research
       goal. Wachter et al. (2017) and Barocas et al. (2020) discuss how counterfactuals differ
       from principal reasons.
       EU AI Act, Annex III 5(b): AI that evaluates creditworthiness or credit scores of natural
       persons is high-risk, with an exception for AI used to detect financial fraud.

  So Part A is a legal duty and Part B is a design goal. Your paper can argue that lenders who
  choose to offer recourse should make it durable.

  1.2 Two pathologies the experiments must demonstrate (hypotheses, not facts)
   1. Twin features. Financial data contains blocks of features measuring one economic
      concept (revolving utilization, balance-to-limit, share of high-utilization trades).
       Models and explainers split credit among them arbitrarily. Effects: explainers appear to
       disagree at the feature level; a top-K list fills with copies of one concept; a
       counterfactual that changes one twin and freezes the other lands off the data
       manifold.
   2. Non-monotonic pockets. Deep trees fit noise in month t and create small regions
      where more debt lowers predicted risk. Effects: sign-violating attributions;
      counterfactual search, which hunts for the nearest point across the boundary, can land
       inside a pocket that disappears after retraining.

  1.4 What is and is not novel
       Robust recourse for tree ensembles already exists (Dutta et al., ICML 2022), as does
       probabilistically robust recourse (Pawelczyk et al., ICLR 2023). The original claim that
       nobody has addressed tree ensembles is too strong.
       A defensible claim, subject to a final literature search: a systematic audit of how de-
       redundancy plus partial monotonicity affect recourse durability under real retraining,
       combined with coalition-level explainer consensus, on credit data.
       The consensus method must be shown to beat naive RRF, single explainers, mRMR and
       Lasso on VIF, PR-AUC and recourse durability. Until Tables 1–5 are filled, none of this is
       a result.

  Part 3. The ten concepts, built from the ground up
  This part assumes you know only that SHAP and LIME give each feature a number saying
  how much it mattered for one prediction. Everything else is built here from small numbers
  you can check by hand. Each concept follows one rhythm: the idea in plain words, a tiny
  worked example, then what it means for the paper.

  The numbers in the worked examples are invented for teaching. They are not results.

  Suggested path: read Concept 0 once, then Concepts 3 to 5 (the heart of the method),
  then the rest in order. Keep a pencil nearby and redo each calculation.


  Concept 0. The toolkit: eight small ideas everything else rests on
  If any of these is already obvious, skim it. The rest of Part 3 uses all of them.

  0.1 A model is a function from features to a risk score. A borrower is a list of numbers, for
  example x = (revolving utilization 82%, inquiries in the last 6 months 3, delinquencies 0).
  The model f turns that list into one number between 0 and 1, the predicted risk (a
  probability of default). Training means picking f so its scores match past outcomes.
  Explaining means answering one question: how much did each input push this particular
  score up or down?

  0.2 Probability, odds and log-odds. Odds = p / (1 − p). Log-odds = ln(odds), also called the
  logit. The sigmoid σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z)) converts log-odds back to a probability.


     Probability p             Odds            Log-odds

     0.50                      1               0

     0.80                      4               1.386

     0.20                      0.25            −1.386

     0.95                      19              2.944

     0.05                      0.0526          −2.944


  Why this matters here: log-odds can be any real number and they add up. Boosted trees
  and EBMs build their score as a sum in log-odds, and SHAP values for them are measured

  Concept 1. Classification, thresholds and the right metrics
  The task. Each row is a borrower or transaction: features x and a label y, where y = 1
  means bad (default or fraud) and y = 0 means good. The model outputs a risk f(x)
  between 0 and 1. A threshold τ turns the score into a decision: reject if f(x) > τ, approve if
  f(x) ≤ τ. Throughout this manual lower risk is better, so approval means f ≤ τ.

  Four outcomes. Every decision falls into one cell.


                                  Truly bad (y = 1)              Truly good (y = 0)

     Model rejects                True positive (TP): bad one    False positive (FP): good customer
                                  caught                         wrongly rejected

     Model                        False negative (FN): bad one   True negative (TN): good customer
     approves                     missed                         correctly approved


  Three rates come from these counts: recall (TPR) = TP / (TP + FN), the share of bad cases
  caught; false positive rate (FPR) = FP / (FP + TN), the share of good cases wrongly
  rejected; precision = TP / (TP + FP), the share of rejections that were truly bad.

  Why plain accuracy fails. If 1% of transactions are fraud, a model that always says
  legitimate is 99% accurate and catches nothing. With rare bad outcomes you need
  metrics that look at the rare class.

  Worked example. 100,000 transactions: 1,000 fraud and 99,000 legitimate. A model flags
  2,000 legitimate ones and catches 800 fraud cases.

       FP = 2,000 and TN = 97,000, so FPR = 2,000 / 99,000 = 2.02%.
       TPR = 800 / 1,000 = 80%.



                                                                                                      Page 16 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




         Precision = 800 / 2,800 = 28.6%, so 71.4% of flagged customers are innocent.

  The ROC curve plots TPR against FPR. Because the good class is huge, even 2,000 false
  alarms is only a 2% FPR, so ROC-AUC can look excellent while most alerts are wrong.
  Precision is the number that reveals it.

  PR-AUC by hand. Sort six cases by score from highest to lowest. At each cutoff n (flag the
  top n), compute precision and recall. Suppose the labels, in score order, are 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1.


     Flag top n           Label added              TP so far   Precision   Recall

     1                    1                        1           1.000       0.333

     2                    0                        1           0.500       0.333

     3                    1                        2           0.667       0.667

     4                    0                        2           0.500       0.667

     5                    0                        2           0.400       0.667

     6                    1                        3           0.500       1.000


  Average precision adds up precision times the increase in recall at each step: AP = 0.333
  × 1.000 + 0.333 × 0.667 + 0.333 × 0.500 = 0.333 + 0.222 + 0.167 = 0.722. A useless model
  scores about the prevalence (here 3 of 6 = 0.5; for 1% fraud, 0.01), not 0.5 as ROC-AUC
  would suggest. That is why a PR-AUC of 0.30 can be excellent when prevalence is 1%.

  Recall at a fixed FPR. A bank often decides in advance how many good customers it will
  tolerate inconveniencing, say 1%. With 99,000 good customers that is 990 false alarms. Set
  the threshold so exactly 1% of good cases score above it, then read off recall. This is the
  operational question: how many bad cases do we catch for a fixed customer-friction
  budget? Report both 1% and 5%.

  Brier score and calibration. Brier = average of (predicted probability − outcome)². For
  predictions 0.9, 0.2, 0.7 and outcomes 1, 0, 1: (0.1² + 0.2² + 0.3²) / 3 = (0.01 + 0.04 + 0.09) / 3 =
  0.047. Lower is better. A model is calibrated if, among borrowers given 20%, about 20%
  really default. Calibration matters because lenders compute expected loss = PD × LGD ×
  EAD (probability of default, loss given default, exposure). With PD 0.05, LGD 0.45 and
  EAD $10,000, expected loss is $225. If the model reports PD 0.10 instead, the lender
  provisions $450. Ranking quality (AUC) does not catch this; calibration does.




                                                                                                Page 17 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




  A trap when you subsample. Suppose you keep only a fraction r of the good cases to
  speed up training. The model's probabilities are then inflated. With r = 0.1, a subsample
  score p_s = 0.5 corresponds to a true probability of 0.1 × 0.5 / (0.1 × 0.5 + 0.5) = 0.091. Fix it
  with p = r p_s / (r p_s + 1 − p_s) (Dal Pozzolo et al. 2015), or train on the subsample and
  evaluate on the full data.

  What this means for the paper. Report PR-AUC, Recall at 1% and 5% FPR, and Brier score
  in every table. Never report accuracy alone.


  Concept 2. How the models make a prediction
  You cannot understand an explanation without knowing what is being explained. Three
  model types appear in this project.

  2.1 A decision tree is a flowchart. It asks yes/no questions about the features and ends in
  a leaf holding a number (here a log-odds contribution).

       Is utilization above 70%?
             Yes: is inquiries above 2? Yes gives leaf +1.2; no gives leaf +0.5.
             No: leaf −0.8.

  A borrower with utilization 82% and 3 inquiries lands in the +1.2 leaf. Two properties
  matter later. First, a tree is a step function: its output is flat between thresholds and
  jumps at them. Second, each leaf value is learned from the training rows that fall into it. A
  leaf built from only four noisy rows has a noisy value; this is the origin of the “pockets” in
  Part 1.

  2.2 A random forest averages many deep trees, each trained on a random resample of the
  data. Averaging reduces noise.

  2.3 Gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM) builds trees one after another, each
  correcting what the previous ones got wrong. Start with a base score. Compute each
  row's leftover error: for a defaulted borrower (y = 1) currently scored 0.4, the leftover is y
  − p = 0.6. Fit a small tree to those leftovers, add it with a small step size η (say 0.05), and
  repeat a few hundred times. The final score is the sum of all trees in log-odds. Because a
  tree that asks k questions can combine k features in one rule, the sum mixes features in
  tangled ways. There is no single curve you can point to and say: this is what utilization
  does. That is why such a model is called a black box.

  2.4 The Explainable Boosting Machine (EBM) gives every feature its own curve. It is still
  boosting, but each small tree may use one feature only, and the algorithm visits the
  features in turn, many times, with a very small step size. At the end, all trees that used



                                                                                              Page 18 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




  utilization are added into one lookup table f_util. The prediction is simply

  log-odds = intercept + f_util(utilization) + f_delinq(delinquencies) + f_inq(inquiries)

  and you can plot each f as a curve. This is a glass box: the model is its own explanation.

  Worked example (invented numbers). Each curve below also has its average value over
  all borrowers.


     Term                        Borrower's value            Curve gives   Average of the curve

     Intercept                   —                           −1.0          —

     Utilization                 82%                         +1.2          +0.5

     Delinquencies               0                           −0.3          −0.1

     Inquiries                   3                           +0.4          +0.2


       Borrower's log-odds = −1.0 + 1.2 − 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.3, so risk = σ(0.3) = 0.574.
       The average borrower has log-odds −1.0 + 0.5 − 0.1 + 0.2 = −0.4, so risk = σ(−0.4) =
       0.401.
       The gap is 0.3 − (−0.4) = +0.7. Split it by feature, using φ_j = f_j(x_j) − average of f_j:
       utilization +0.7, delinquencies −0.2, inquiries +0.2. These add to +0.7 exactly.

  That is an exact explanation with no sampling and no approximation. (The interpret
  package centres the curves differently from this toy, but φ_j is the same.)

  2.5 Pairwise interactions. An EBM can add terms f_ij(x_i, x_j) for selected pairs. They
  improve accuracy but break the one-curve-per-feature picture and, as Concept 8 shows,
  they spoil monotonicity guarantees. For the monotone model in this project set
  interactions = 0.

  2.6 The twin problem, inside an EBM. Suppose utilization (x_1) and balance-to-limit (x_2)
  are twins. Run the EBM twice with different random seeds. Run A may put most of the
  signal in f_1 (curve range 1.4) and little in f_2 (range 0.6). Run B may do the reverse. Both
  runs make nearly identical predictions, because the features carry the same information.
  Yet someone reading one curve alone would call utilization important in run A and
  unimportant in run B. The honest statement is about the pair, which is exactly what a
  coalition is.

  Concept 3. How SHAP, LIME and permutation importance really work
  3.1 The question. A model scored this borrower 0.574 while the average borrower scores
  0.401. An explainer splits that gap among the features. Different explainers use different
  rules for the split, and the rules give different answers when features are correlated. To
  see why, you need to see each rule in action.

  3.2 Shapley values: fair credit by averaging over orderings. Imagine features joining a
  team one at a time. When a feature joins, record how much the team's score rises: its
  marginal contribution. The result depends on who joined before it, so average over every
  possible joining order.

  Tiny game with two players. The team scores v(nobody) = 0, v(A alone) = 10, v(B alone) =
  20, v(A and B) = 50. The players interact: together they do more than the sum.


     Joining order              A gets                   B gets

     A first, then B            10                       50 − 10 = 40

     B first, then A            50 − 20 = 30             20

     Average                    20                       30


  The credits sum to 50, the full team score. These averages are the Shapley values. Four
  plain-language fairness rules pin them down uniquely: credits sum to the total
  (efficiency); identical players get equal credit (symmetry); a player who adds nothing ever
  gets zero (null player); and credits from two games add up (additivity). With d features
  there are d! orderings (23! for HELOC), so practical tools use shortcuts. TreeSHAP
  computes the exact answer for tree models quickly.

  3.3 Turning a model into a game. The team score of a set S of features is the model's
  average output when the features in S are fixed at this borrower's values and the other
  features are filled in with draws from a background dataset. With nothing fixed, you get
  the average prediction; with everything fixed, the borrower's own prediction.

  Worked example with a simple model f = 3·x1 + 2·x2. Background averages are x1 = 1 and x2
  = 2. The borrower has x1 = 2 and x2 = 5.


                                                                                       Page 20 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




         Nothing fixed: v = 3·1 + 2·2 = 7 (the average prediction).
         Only x1 fixed: v = 3·2 + 2·2 = 10. Only x2 fixed: v = 3·1 + 2·5 = 13.
         Both fixed: v = 3·2 + 2·5 = 16 (the borrower's prediction).
         φ_1 = ½[(10 − 7) + (16 − 13)] = 3 and φ_2 = ½[(13 − 7) + (16 − 10)] = 6.

  Check: 7 + 3 + 6 = 16. For a linear model, SHAP is just coefficient × (value − average): 3×1
  and 2×3. The same recipe works for any model; only the arithmetic gets harder.

  3.4 Why twins break feature-level explanations. Take two features that are perfect twins
  in the real data (x2 always equals x1). Consider two models that predict identically on real
  borrowers:

         Model A: f = 2·x1 + 2·x2.
         Model B: f = 4·x1 + 0·x2.

  Let the averages be 1 and 1, and the borrower have x1 = x2 = 3.


     Model          φ for x1                φ for x2         Ranking says                      Twin total

     A              2·(3 − 1) = 4           4                x1 and x2 tie                     8

     B              4·(3 − 1) = 8           0                x1 is everything, x2 irrelevant   8


  Both explanations are faithful to their model. But a model trained on twin data has no way
  to prefer A over B, so the split between twins is arbitrary, and two trained trees, two
  seeds or two explainers can land on different splits. What is stable is the total for the pair
  (8). That total is the idea behind coalitions.

  3.5 The SHAP variants you will use.

         Interventional TreeSHAP ( shap.TreeExplainer with a background dataset): the
         recipe of 3.3, exactly, for trees. Faithful to the model, even where it evaluates
         unrealistic feature combinations.
         Path-dependent TreeSHAP: uses how many training rows went down each branch to
         approximate conditional averages. Needs no background; not true conditional SHAP.
         PartitionExplainer: respects a hierarchy of feature clusters and yields Owen-type
         values (Concept 5). Slow, so subsample rows.

  3.6 LIME from scratch. LIME explains one borrower by fitting a simple line locally. Five
  steps: (1) take the borrower; (2) create many slightly altered copies; (3) ask the model to
  score every copy; (4) give each copy a weight that shrinks with its distance from the




                                                                                                            Page 21 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




  borrower, controlled by a kernel width; (5) fit a weighted linear regression and read its
  coefficients as the explanation.

  The coefficient is a slope. For a tree, which is a step function, the slope depends entirely
  on how wide the neighbourhood is. Toy case: f(x) = 0 for x < 5 and 1 for x ≥ 5, and the
  borrower sits at 4.9.

       Narrow neighbourhood: copies at 4.8 and 4.9 are all scored 0, so the fitted slope is 0.
       LIME says x does not matter.
       Wider neighbourhood: copies at 4.5, 4.9, 5.1, 5.5 score 0, 0, 1, 1. With equal weights the
       regression slope is Sum[(x − 5)(y − 0.5)] / Sum[(x − 5)²] = 0.60 / 0.52 = 1.15. LIME says x
       matters a lot.

  Same model, same borrower, different answers. Random sampling adds more noise. Two
  practical points: in the lime package set discretize_continuous = False and
  sample_around_instance = True to get local slopes; and a slope is not a contribution. To
  compare with SHAP, convert: contribution = slope × (value − reference value).

  3.7 Permutation importance (PFI). Shuffle one column on validation data so its link to the
  outcome is broken, then see how much the model's score drops. Large drop means the
  model leans on that feature. It is a global measure: one number per feature, not per
  borrower.

  Illustrative numbers: baseline PR-AUC 0.60. Shuffle x1 alone: 0.57, a drop of only 0.03,
  because its twin x2 still carries the information. Shuffle x1 and x2 together, with the same
  row shuffle: 0.35, a drop of 0.25. Single-column PFI therefore understates twins; shuffling
  the whole group together tells the truth about the concept.

  Concept 4. Finding the twins: correlation, clustering and VIF
  4.1 Why automate it. The FICO challenge winners grouped features into subscales by
  hand (Chen et al. 2018). That is subjective and does not transfer to a new dataset. We
  want a rule that finds twin groups from the training data alone.

  4.2 Turn correlation into distance. Use the Spearman correlation ρ_S between two
  columns (Concept 0.5). Define the distance D = 1 − abs(ρ_S). Two features that move
  together almost perfectly have D near 0; unrelated features have D near 1. Using the
  absolute value means a strong negative relationship also counts as twins.

  4.3 Hierarchical clustering by hand. Start with each feature as its own cluster and
  repeatedly merge the two closest clusters. Four features A, B, C, D with these absolute
  correlations:


     Pair        abs(ρ_S)            Distance D

     A–B         0.92                0.08

     C–D         0.85                0.15

     A–C         0.15                0.85

     A–D         0.10                0.90

     Pair        abs(ρ_S)            Distance D

     B–C         0.20                0.80

     B–D         0.12                0.88


  With complete linkage the distance between two clusters is the largest distance between
  any member of one and any member of the other.

   1. Smallest distance is A–B at 0.08. Merge into {A, B} at height 0.08.
   2. Next smallest is C–D at 0.15. Merge into {C, D} at height 0.15.
   3. Distance between {A, B} and {C, D} is the largest cross distance, max(0.85, 0.90, 0.80,
      0.88) = 0.90. Merge at height 0.90.

  Now cut the tree at height γ = 1 − ρ_max. With ρ_max = 0.70, γ = 0.30. Keep only merges at
  heights up to 0.30. That leaves the two coalitions {A, B} and {C, D}. These are the concept
  coalitions. No labels were used; only the training features.

  4.4 What the cut guarantees, and what it does not. With complete linkage, every pair
  inside a cluster has D ≤ γ, so abs(ρ_S) ≥ ρ_max. That is a real guarantee. Nothing is
  promised about pairs in different clusters. Example with ρ_max = 0.70 (γ = 0.30): abs(ρ) is
  0.90 for A–B, 0.75 for B–C and 0.40 for A–C, so the distances are 0.10, 0.25 and 0.60.

       Complete linkage merges A and B at 0.10. Then {A, B} versus C is max(0.60, 0.25) = 0.60,
       above 0.30, so C stays separate. Result: {A, B} and {C}. But B and C have correlation
       0.75. Pick B from the first cluster and C from the second, and you have selected two
       highly correlated features.
       Single linkage uses the smallest cross distance: min(0.60, 0.25) = 0.25, below 0.30, so it
       merges all three into one cluster even though A and C are only 0.40 correlated. This is
       called chaining.

  4.5 VIF from scratch. Regress feature j on the other selected features. The regression's R²
  is the share of j's variation the others already explain. Then

  VIF_j = 1 / (1 − R²).

  If R² = 0.5, VIF = 2. If R² = 0.9, VIF = 10. The name says what it does: the variance of j's
  estimated coefficient in a regression is multiplied by this factor, so coefficients become
  unstable and signs can flip. For just two features, R² equals the squared correlation, so VIF
  = 1 / (1 − ρ²):


     Correlation ρ               0.5        0.7         0.9    0.94

     VIF of the pair             1.33       1.96        5.26   8.59


  Rules of thumb: above 5 is worrying, above 10 is severe. Notice that ρ = 0.7 gives a pair VIF
  just under 2. That is where the target “VIF below 2” comes from. With three or more
  selected features the combined effect can push VIF higher than any single pair suggests.

  4.6 Practicalities. Cluster on Spearman (it handles skewed financial data), but VIF is a
  linear measure, so report both a Spearman maximum pairwise correlation and the VIF.
  Handle special codes such as −9 before computing any correlation. A column that is
  constant has no defined correlation; drop it.

  What this means for the paper. The defensible statement is: coalitions are found without
  labels, on training data only; every pair inside a coalition has abs(ρ_S) at least ρ_max; and
  the VIF of every selected subset is measured and reported, not assumed.


  Concept 5. From Shapley to Owen: crediting whole concepts
  5.1 The idea. Shapley's rule treats every feature as free to join in any order. Twins are not
  free: they carry one concept and arrive as a bloc. Guillermo Owen (1977) changed the rule
  so that blocks enter together, in games with “a priori unions”. Credit is shared in two
  steps: first among the blocks, then among the members of each block.

  5.2 A six-ordering example. Players A and B are twins (one coalition) and C is
  independent (a second coalition). Team scores: v(A) = 30, v(B) = 30, v(C) = 40, v(A,B) = 40
  (twins overlap, so together barely more than one alone), v(A,C) = 70, v(B,C) = 70, and
  v(A,B,C) = 100. The extra 100 − (40 + 40) = 20 is synergy between the two concepts.




                                                                                                 Page 25 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




  Shapley: all 6 orders. Each row shows what each player adds when it joins in that order.


     Order              A adds          B adds          C adds

     A, B, C            30              10              60

     A, C, B            30              30              40

     B, A, C            10              30              60

     B, C, A            30              30              40

     C, A, B            30              30              40

     C, B, A            30              30              40

     Average            26.67           26.67           46.67


  The three averages sum to 100. Adding the twins gives 26.67 + 26.67 = 53.33.

  Owen: only orders where A and B stay together. The orders A,C,B and B,C,A put C
  between the twins, so Owen drops them. Four orders remain: A,B,C; B,A,C; C,A,B; C,B,A.

       A: (30 + 10 + 30 + 30) / 4 = 25. B: (10 + 30 + 30 + 30) / 4 = 25. C: (60 + 60 + 40 + 40) / 4 =
       50.
       The twins together get 25 + 25 = 50.

  Check against the block-level game. Treat the twin block as one player worth 40 alone, C
  worth 40 alone, and both worth 100. Shapley for that two-player game gives the twin block
  ½·40 + ½·(100 − 40) = 50. This matches the Owen total for the twins, which is Owen's
  quotient efficiency property: the members of a coalition together receive exactly what
  the coalition would get as a single player.

  The lesson. Summing ordinary Shapley values over the twins gave 53.33. The true coalition
  value is 50. They differ because the two concepts interact (the 20 synergy gets split
  differently). If there were no synergy, so v(A,B,C) = 80, both methods give the twins 40.
  So:

       For a model that is additive across coalitions (an EBM with interactions = 0), summing
       ordinary attributions inside a coalition equals the Owen coalition value exactly.
       For tree ensembles with interactions between concepts, the sum is an approximation.
       The original documents called it the Owen value; call it a coalition-sum attribution
       instead.

  any set of other coalitions (q of them), S any set of other members of C_k (s of them), and
  U(Q) the union of the coalitions in Q:

  Owen value of j = Sum over Q and S of [ q!(M − q − 1)!/M! ] × [ s!(c − s − 1)!/c! ] × ( v(U(Q) ∪ S
  ∪ {j}) − v(U(Q) ∪ S) ).

  The first bracket averages over the order in which coalitions arrive; the second averages
  over the order of members inside the coalition. The Shapley value uses a single bracket
  over all features.

  5.4 What we actually compute. For borrower x, add up the explainer's credit over the
  coalition: Φ_m(x) = sum of φ_j(x) for j in C_m. The coalition's global importance is the
  average over N borrowers of the absolute value of that sum:

  I(C_m) = (1/N) × Sum over borrowers of abs( Φ_m(x) ).

  5.5 Why the absolute value goes outside the sum. Suppose a twin pair receives φ_1 =
  +5.0 and φ_2 = −4.8 on some borrower, the classic cancellation when two near-identical
  features are fitted together. Taking absolute values first gives 9.8 and suggests a huge
  concept. The actual net push of the concept on the prediction is abs(5.0 − 4.8) = 0.2.
  Caveat: a coalition may hold forces that genuinely oppose each other and offset on some
  borrowers. So report the sum of absolute values as a sensitivity check beside the net
  version.

  Concept 6. Building the consensus: weighted rank fusion and one representative
  per concept
  6.1 The goal. We have several explainers, each producing its own ranking. We want one
  ranking of concepts we can trust, then K concrete features, one per top concept, to feed
  a compact model. Two decisions follow: how to merge rankings (Stage 1) and how to pick a
  representative inside a concept (Stage 2).

  6.2 Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) by hand. Each explainer gives a rank r to each item. An
  item's fused score is the sum, over explainers, of w / (k0 + r), where w is the explainer's
  weight and k0 is a smoothing constant. A high rank (small r) gives a bigger term. Three
  explainers with equal weights 1/3 rank four coalitions:


    Coalition Rank by explainer 1 Explainer 2 Explainer 3 Fused score, k0 = 60 Fused score, k0 = 1

    C1              1                             2          1   0.01631        0.4444

    C2              2                             1          3   0.01613        0.3611

    C3              3                             3          2   0.01596        0.2778

    C4              4                             4          4   0.01563        0.2000


  Example calculation, C1 with k0 = 60: (1/3) × (1/61 + 1/62 + 1/61) = (1/3) × 0.04892 = 0.01631.
  With k0 = 1: (1/3) × (1/2 + 1/3 + 1/2) = 0.4444.

  Both settings give the same order here, C1, C2, C3, C4. The difference is spread. With k0 =
  60 the four scores differ by only about 4%, because 1/61 and 1/64 are nearly equal. RRF
  with k0 = 60 was designed for ranking thousands of documents. With only 5 to 15
  coalitions, a small k0 such as 1 to 10 makes rank differences matter more. That is why the
  sensitivity study sweeps k0.

  6.3 Why weight the explainers. Some explainers may point at features the model barely
  uses. A faithfulness test checks this. For each explainer, set its top-k features to typical
  (median) values and measure how much the model's prediction changes. A big change

  features (PGI). Invented numbers: explainer 1 changes predictions by 0.12 on average,
  explainer 2 by 0.05, explainer 3 by 0.13. Weights are these divided by their total 0.30:


                            PGI         Weight

  Concept 7. Measuring agreement and sign violations
  Two questions need numbers. Do two explainers agree on which features or concepts
  matter? And does an explanation ever contradict what finance tells us must be true?

  7.1 Agreement between two rankings. Compute each metric twice: on the d raw features
  and on the M coalitions.

       Overlap@k. The share of the top k that both lists share. Top 3 of list A is {a, b, c}; top 3
       of list B is {b, a, g}. They share a and b, so Overlap@3 = 2/3 = 0.667. Simple, but it ignores
       order inside the top k.
       Kendall τ. Look at every pair of items and ask whether both lists order the pair the
       same way. Lists (a, b, c, d) and (b, a, c, d) have 6 pairs; 5 are ordered the same and 1 (a
       versus b) is reversed. τ = (5 − 1) / 6 = 0.667. It weighs a swap at the bottom as much as a
       swap at the top.
       Rank-biased overlap (RBO). Like Overlap, but it gives the top ranks more weight,
       controlled by p (0.85 puts about half the weight on ranks 1 to 4 or 5). The version to use
       for finite lists is

  RBO_ext = (X_l / l) · p^l + ((1 − p) / p) · Sum over d = 1..l of (X_d / d) · p^d,

  where X_d is the number of items the two lists share within their top d, and l is the list
  length.

  RBO_ext by hand. Lists (a, b, c) and (a, c, b), p = 0.85.

     Depth d           Shared in top d (X_d)                 X_d / d   p^d      Term (X_d / d) × p^d

     1                 1                                     1.000     0.8500   0.8500

     2                 1                                     0.500     0.7225   0.3613

     3                 3                                     1.000     0.6141   0.6141


  The terms sum to 1.8254. Multiply by (1 − p) / p = 0.1765 to get 0.3221. Add the tail term

  Subtracting luck. If you rank 23 items twice at random, the expected Overlap@5 is 5 / 23 =

  7.2 Sign conflicts (ESCR). Two explainers can agree a feature matters yet disagree on
  direction: explainer A gives utilization +0.30 (raises risk) and explainer B gives −0.10. The
  explanation sign conflict rate is the share of top-k features whose signs differ between
  two explainers on the same borrower. Convert LIME's slope to a contribution (slope ×
  (value − reference)) first, or the signs are not comparable with SHAP. Write the exact
  definition in your paper; Stow (2026) is a thin source for it.

  7.3 Economic sign violations (ESVR). Finance gives each feature a known direction s_j:
  more utilization, more inquiries and more delinquencies raise risk (s = +1); longer clean
  history lowers it (s = −1). A violation is an explanation saying the opposite: that a
  borrower's unusually high utilization reduced their risk.

  Why the reference point matters. Suppose utilization has median 40%, and the borrower
  is at 45%, above the median. The model's utilization curve is increasing, so it is monotone.
  Its values: f_util(40%) = 0.2 and f_util(45%) = 0.3. A few borrowers with very high
  utilization pull the curve's average up to 0.5.

         Measured against the average: φ = 0.3 − 0.5 = −0.2. Above-median utilization appears
         to lower risk. It looks like a violation, yet the model is perfectly monotone.
         Measured against the median: φ = 0.3 − 0.2 = +0.1. No violation.




                                                                                                       Page 31 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




  So define the audit with a median reference. Let m be the median borrower and u = s_j ×
  (x_j − m_j), the displacement in the risky direction. Let φ_j be the attribution measured
  from m, positive when it raises risk. A violation is u × φ_j < 0. ESVR is violations divided by
  pairs tested.

  Proposition (zero violations). If the model is monotone in every constrained feature,
  ESVR is 0 under median-reference SHAP.

  Why. The SHAP value for j is a weighted average of terms, each comparing the model on
  two inputs that differ only in feature j (the borrower's x_j against the median m_j). If the
  model never decreases when x_j moves toward risk, each term has the sign of u or is zero,
  so the weighted average cannot have the opposite sign. For an additive EBM there is a
  single term, f_j(x_j) − f_j(m_j).

  An unconstrained model with a noisy pocket can break this. If its curve dips so that a
  borrower at 82% utilization gets φ = −0.1 against the median, that is a violation. The claim
  in the originals that unconstrained models commit 8–22% violations is a hypothesis until
  you measure it.

  7.4 Stability. Retrain the model on bootstrap resamples and compute the RBO between
  the top-K lists from different resamples. High values mean the explanation does not
  depend on which rows the model happened to see.

  What this means for the paper. Report agreement as observed minus chance, using
  RBO_ext. State the ESVR baseline whenever you quote an ESVR figure. Use the zero-
  violation proposition as a sanity check on your implementation: if a monotone model
  shows violations under median-reference attributions, you have a bug.


  Concept 8. Monotonicity: forcing the model to respect common sense
  8.1 What it means. A model is monotone in a feature if moving that feature one way, with
  everything else held fixed, can never move the prediction the wrong way. For credit risk,
  more revolving utilization can never lower predicted risk (direction s = +1); a longer time
  since last delinquency can never raise it (s = −1). A feature with s = 0 is left free. “Partially
  monotone” means monotone in the constrained features only.

  8.2 Why unconstrained trees break it. Trees fit whatever the training rows say. Imagine
  the model's utilization curve, in six bins from low to high utilization, comes out as risk
  contributions

  0.10, 0.40, 0.30, 0.60, 0.50, 0.90.




                                                                                              Page 32 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




  The third bin (0.30) is lower than the second (0.40), and the fifth (0.50) is lower than the
  fourth (0.60). Those dips are pockets, probably caused by a handful of noisy rows. A
  borrower in bin 3 is told that having more utilization than a bin-2 borrower made them
  safer. SHAP or LIME would faithfully report that. A counterfactual optimiser would love it:
  the nearest way to look less risky is to step into a dip.

  8.3 The repair by hand (pool adjacent violators). Find neighbours that break the order,
  replace both by their average, and repeat until the sequence never decreases. Equal
  weights here (in practice bins are weighted by their row counts).

   1. 0.40 then 0.30 is a violation. Average = 0.35. The sequence is now 0.10, 0.35, 0.35, 0.60,
       0.50, 0.90.
   2. 0.60 then 0.50 is a violation. Average = 0.55. The sequence is now 0.10, 0.35, 0.35, 0.55,
      0.55, 0.90.
   3. No decreases remain.

  This is the projection the EBM's monotonize step performs on each shape function. It
  changes the curve as little as the order requirement allows.

  8.4 How boosted trees enforce it. At a split on a feature with s = +1, the builder requires
  every leaf value in the left child (lower feature values) to be no higher than every leaf
  value in the right child. A sum of trees that each respect this is monotone. The sigmoid is
  increasing, so the probability is monotone too. In code: LightGBM and XGBoost take a
   monotone_constraints list with +1, −1 or 0 for each feature.

  8.5 How the EBM does it. Use interactions = 0, fit, then make each constrained shape
  function monotone ( monotonize , an isotonic projection; a monotone_constraints
  argument also exists). Pair terms f_ij(x_i, x_j) are not constrained this way, which is why
  they are switched off. Always plot every shape function afterward. The
   monotone_violation_rate helper in Part 7 sweeps one feature and counts borrowers
  whose predicted risk moves the wrong way.

  8.6 Why monotonicity helps recourse. The acceptance region is the set of feature values
  with risk at or below the threshold. Consider a borrower at utilization 82% who is
  rejected.

       Unconstrained model: the acceptance region has an island at utilization 78–82% (a
       pocket). The nearest accepted point is 80%, which costs only 2 points. DiCE-style
       search happily returns it. After the bank retrains next month, the island vanishes with
       the noisy rows that created it, and the borrower is rejected again.




                                                                                            Page 33 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




       Monotone model: if 45% is accepted, then so is every lower value. The only accepted
       points lie beyond a clean boundary, say 45%. The recourse costs 37 points but is real.

  Proposition 2 (no islands along constrained coordinates). Let I be the constrained
  features. If a point x' is accepted and x'' agrees with x' outside I but is no riskier in every
  constrained feature, then x'' is also accepted. Reason: change one constrained coordinate
  at a time toward lower risk; the model never increases. Features with s = 0 can still hold
  islands.

  Proposition 2′ (why a margin helps). Suppose retraining moves each score near the
  counterfactual by at most δ. If the counterfactual's risk is at most τ − δ, it is still accepted
  after retraining. This is trivial, and it is the real mechanism. Monotonicity removes
  pockets, which should make δ smaller and smoother; the margin absorbs what remains.
  Whether δ really is smaller is a question for Table 4, not an assumption.

  8.7 Honest limits. Monotone directions are domain assumptions: take them from the
  official data dictionary and document them. A constrained model can fit slightly worse,
  and Table 3 measures that price. Special codes such as −9 sit at the low end of a numeric
  column and would be treated as the best (or worst) value, so recode them first.
  Monotonicity is a property of the model you have today; it does not by itself say anything
  about next month's model.

  What this means for the paper. State Propositions 2 and 2′ exactly as above and claim
  durability only to the extent that Table 4 supports it.


  Concept 9. Recourse and the safety margin
  9.1 What a counterfactual is. A counterfactual explanation answers: what is the smallest
  change to this applicant that would flip the decision? Example: risk 0.62 against a
  threshold τ = 0.50, with utilization 82% and 3 inquiries. Lowering utilization to 45% might
  bring risk to 0.49. That new point is the counterfactual, and the change is the recourse.

  9.2 Rules for what may change. Features fall into groups. Immutable: age, credit history
  already written. Directional: a delinquency count can only stay or fall, never fall instantly.
  Time-gated: inquiries and months since delinquency improve only as time passes.
  Actionable: utilization can be paid down. The set of allowed changes is written A(x).
  Search only inside A(x).

  9.3 Measuring effort with MAD. Features have different units, so each change is divided
  by a scale before adding up. The median absolute deviation (MAD) is the median of how
  far values sit from their median. Utilization values 20, 30, 40, 50, 90: median 40; distances
  from it are 20, 10, 0, 10, 50; the median of those is 10. Cutting utilization from 82% to 45%


                                                                                            Page 34 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




  is 37 points, or 3.7 MAD units. If a feature's MAD is 0 (a count that is mostly zero), use its
  mean absolute deviation instead. Cost is the sum over changed features: add a 10-point
  cut in installment burden with scale 5, which costs 2.0, and the total is 5.7.

  9.4 Three ways recourse fails after retraining.

   1. Boundary hugging. The search returns a point at 0.499 when τ = 0.500. Next month's
      model moves scores by +0.03 and the applicant is at 0.529, rejected.
   2. Pockets. The search finds an island of acceptance created by noisy rows (Concept 8).
         Retraining removes it.
   3. Twin mismatch. The search lowers utilization and leaves its twin balance-to-limit
      untouched. No real borrower looks like that, and a retrained model may rely on the twin
         you did not change.

  9.5 Measuring durability. Temporal recourse validity (TRV) is the share of counterfactuals
  still approved by the model retrained Δt months later. Five applicants receive
  counterfactuals; under the retrained model four are approved, so TRV = 4 / 5 = 80%. Two
  things must be reported with it. Coverage: for how many rejected applicants did a
  counterfactual exist at all? The threshold rule: if the bank resets τ each month to hold its
  approval rate constant, the same score can pass or fail depending on the new τ. State the
  rule, and report at least two.

  9.6 The safety margin, by hand. Idea: do not stop at the boundary; aim for a score
  comfortably below it, with the cushion sized by how much scores really moved in past
  retrainings.

  Step 1. Take applicants near the boundary and compare their threshold-relative score
  under two consecutive retrained models. Ten shifts (positive means riskier): +0.02, −0.01,
  +0.04, 0.00, +0.03, +0.01, −0.02, +0.05, +0.02, +0.01.

  Step 2. Only increases in risk hurt, so keep the positive part: 0.02, 0, 0.04, 0, 0.03, 0.01, 0,
  0.05, 0.02, 0.01. Sorted: 0, 0, 0, 0.01, 0.01, 0.02, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04, 0.05.

  Step 3. Pick the level α. The cushion q̂ is the ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉-th smallest of the n values.


     α           ⌈(10 + 1)(1 − α)⌉            Cushion q̂     Safe threshold τ − q̂

     0.10        10                           0.05           0.45

     0.20        9                            0.04           0.46

  Step 4. Accept a counterfactual only if its risk is at most the safe threshold. The point at
  0.49 fails at α = 0.10; utilization 38% giving 0.44 passes. After a +0.03 shift, 0.49 becomes

  What the margin does and does not promise. If future shifts behave like past shifts, a
  counterfactual that clears the margin should fail at the boundary with probability roughly
  α. If the economy changes and shifts become larger, the guarantee is gone. That is why
  the temporal experiment on real months is essential. Do not call this a formal conformal
  guarantee for retrained models. On a static dataset with one training window, obtain
  shifts by retraining on bootstrap resamples and call it a bootstrap-dispersion margin. If
  the safe threshold falls below the lowest risk any allowed change can reach, no safe
  recourse exists; count those cases as failures.

  9.7 Choosing among candidates with the manifold distance. The generator (DiCE)
  returns several valid candidates. Prefer the one that looks like a real approved borrower.
  For each candidate, find its 5 nearest approved borrowers in the calibration set
  (measured in MAD units) and average the distances: d_KNN. Choose the candidate with
  the lowest cost + β × d_KNN. Example with β = 0.5: candidate P costs 4.0 and has d_KNN
  0.8, total 4.4; candidate Q costs 4.0 but changes only one twin, so d_KNN is 3.5, total 5.75.
  P wins. In live lending, labels exist only for accepted loans, which complicates this (reject
  inference); the two credit datasets have all labels.

  9.8 Practicalities. DiCE flips a class at 0.5; wrap the model so its output crosses 0.5
  exactly at the safe threshold (Part 7, ThresholdShifted). Generate more candidates than
  needed and filter afterward. Freeze immutable columns. Never fall back to an unsafe
  candidate; count the applicant as a coverage failure instead.

  What this means for the paper. Present three quantities together for every pipeline:
  validity after retraining, coverage, and cost (plus manifold distance). A method that
  achieves high validity by finding recourse for only a few easy applicants has not solved
  the problem.

  correlated, and an ordinary t-test treats them as independent and finds differences that
  are not there. Second, with several baselines some comparisons look significant by luck.
  Test nine baselines at 5% each and the chance of at least one false alarm is 1 − 0.95⁹ =
  37%. The tools below handle both.

  10.2 Friedman test by hand. Use it when you compare k methods across N datasets or
  windows. Rank the methods within each dataset (1 is best), then average the ranks. Three
  methods on four datasets:


     Dataset                    A           B           C

     D1                         1           2           3

     D2                         1           3           2

     D3                         1           2           3

     D4                         2           1           3

     Average rank               1.25        2.00        2.75


  Formula: χ² = [12N / (k(k + 1))] × [Sum of squared average ranks − k(k + 1)² / 4]. Here 12 × 4 /

  4 × (13.125 − 12) = 4.5.

  The Iman–Davenport version, which Demšar recommends, is F = (N − 1) χ² / (N(k − 1) − χ²)

  freedom. The 5% critical value is about 5.14, so this result is not significant. Lesson: with
  four datasets, even a consistent ordering is not enough. Plan for many windows or
  datasets.

  10.4 Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and why you need enough windows. For paired scores

  many windows you must create.

  10.5 Repeated 2-fold cross-validation (5×2) on static data. Five repetitions of 2-fold CV
  give 10 scores, but they are correlated through shared rows. Corrected tests exist for
  exactly this: Dietterich's 5×2 t, Alpaydın's 5×2 F, and the Nadeau–Bengio corrected
  resampled t-test. The Nadeau–Bengio idea is to inflate the variance by the factor (1/J +
  n_test / n_train) instead of 1/J. With J = 10 and equal halves the factor is 1.1, not 0.1, so a
  naive t of 6.6 shrinks to about 2.0. That is the difference between a headline result and a
  marginal one. For reference, let p_i^(j) be the difference in error between the two
  methods on fold j of repetition i, p̄ _i the mean of its two folds, and s_i² = (p_i^(1) − p̄ _i)² +
  (p_i^(2) − p̄ _i)². Dietterich: t = p_1^(1) / sqrt( (1/5) × Sum of s_i² ), with 5 degrees of
  freedom. Alpaydın: F = Sum over i and j of (p_i^(j))² / ( 2 × Sum of s_i² ), with 10 and 5
  degrees of freedom.

  10.6 Limits you must state. Rolling windows share training months and are
  autocorrelated, so p-values are optimistic. Add bootstrap confidence intervals over
  borrowers and report effect sizes (for example the PR-AUC difference), not only p-values.

  What this means for the paper. Use Friedman plus Holm-corrected Wilcoxon across
  windows and datasets, corrected tests on static 5×2 splits, and say plainly how many
  windows you have and what the smallest attainable p-value is.



  Part 4. Algorithm 1 (corrected)
  Inputs. Training window D_train(t); calibration window D_cal(t); test window D_test(t);
  historical windows for the margin; later windows for evaluation. Feature set D = {1..d};
  threshold ρ_max; budget K; RRF constant k0; monotonicity map s; margin level α;
  candidate count N_cf = 5; manifold weight β.

  Phase 1. Concept coalitions.

   1. Compute the Spearman matrix on D_train only.
   2. Set D(j, l) = 1 − abs(ρ_S(j, l)).
   3. Run complete-linkage clustering and cut at γ = 1 − ρ_max. Record M.


                                                                                                 Page 38 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




  Phase 2. Consensus and selection.

   4. Fit the reference models (GBDT, EBM) on D_train. Extract the explainers on the same
       model for the disagreement audit, and per-instance matrices on D_cal.
   5. Compute faithfulness weights w_e from PGI on D_cal.
   6. For each coalition and explainer, compute the coalition-sum importance I^(e)(C_m);
      use grouped PFI for permutation importance.
   7. Compute RRF(C_m) = Σ_e w_e / (k0 + r_e(C_m)); keep the top K coalitions (apply the
       K > M rule if needed).
   8. Within each, choose the medoid j*. Output S_K*. Measure and report the VIF of S_K*.

  Phase 3. Monotone students.

   9. Restrict to S_K* and the matching sub-vector of s.
 10. Train monotone LightGBM and an EBM with interactions = 0, made monotone, on
     D_train.

  Phase 4. Safe recourse.

 11. Estimate q̂ _α from historical retraining shifts and set τ_safe = τ − q̂ _α.
 12. For each rejected applicant in D_test, generate candidates with DiCE under A(x), keep
     those with f_t(c) ≤ τ_safe, and choose x_cf* by the cost-plus-manifold rule. Log
       applicants for whom none exists.
 13. Return S_K*, glass-box explanations, and x_cf*.

  Leakage rules. Fit clustering, models and margins only on data before the test window.
  D_cal is used both for selection (steps 4–8) and, in static experiments, for calibration; if
  data allow, split it in two. Nested cross-validation must repeat Phases 1–3 inside every
  outer fold.

     # ---------- Module 1: coalition discovery (fit on TRAIN only) ----------
     def discover_concept_coalitions(X_train, rho_max=0.70, method='complete'):
             # complete linkage: every within-cluster pair has abs(rho_S) >= rho_max
             # method='ward' is not valid for 1 - abs(rho); use only as an ablation
             corr = X_train.corr(method='spearman').fillna(0.0).values
             np.fill_diagonal(corr, 1.0)
             abs_corr = np.abs(corr)
             dist = np.clip(1.0 - abs_corr, 0.0, 1.0)
             dist = (dist + dist.T) / 2.0
             np.fill_diagonal(dist, 0.0)
             if X_train.shape[1] == 1:
                 return {1: list(X_train.columns)}, abs_corr
             Z = linkage(squareform(dist, checks=False), method=method)
             labels = fcluster(Z, t=1.0 - rho_max, criterion='distance')
             coalitions = {}
             for col, lab in zip(X_train.columns, labels):
                 coalitions.setdefault(int(lab), []).append(col)
             return coalitions, abs_corr

     # ---------- Agreement metrics ----------
     def rbo_ext(a, b, p=0.85):
         # extrapolated RBO for equal-length lists; identical lists give exactly 1
             l = min(len(a), len(b))
             ov = [len(set(a[:d]) & set(b[:d])) for d in range(1, l + 1)]
             s = sum((ov[d - 1] / d) * p ** d for d in range(1, l + 1))
             return (ov[-1] / l) * p ** l + ((1 - p) / p) * s



     def null_agreement(n_items, k=5, p=0.85, n_draws=1000, seed=0):
             # expected (Overlap@k, RBO_ext) for two random rankings of n_items

                     b = list(rng.permutation(n_items))
                     vals.append((len(set(a[:k]) & set(b[:k])) / k, rbo_ext(a, b, p)))
             return np.mean(vals, axis=0)

     def esvr(phi, X, signs, medians):
         # phi: risk-direction attributions from a MEDIAN-REFERENCE baseline
     (positive raises risk)
             # signs: {feature: -1/0/+1}; medians: training medians
             viol, tested = 0, 0
             for j, s in signs.items():
                     if s == 0:
                         continue
                     u = s * (X[j].values - medians[j])
                     ph = phi[j].values
                     m = u != 0
                     viol += int(np.sum(u[m] * ph[m] < 0))
                     tested += int(m.sum())
             return viol / max(tested, 1)

     def select_conformal_recourse(x_orig, cands, model, X_ref_good, scale,

     def holm(pvals, alpha=0.05):

  8.2 Self-test
  Try each question before reading the answer.

   1. Why can ROC-AUC look excellent while most alerts are false? The FPR denominator
       is the huge count of legitimate cases, so 2,000 false alarms is only 2% FPR. Precision
       (28.6%) exposes the problem.




                                                                                                        Page 56 of 59
COMET-XAI: Corrected Study Manual & Research Specification




   2. Why complete linkage and not Ward on 1 − abs(ρ)? Ward assumes Euclidean
       distances. Complete linkage bounds the largest within-cluster distance by γ, giving
       abs(ρ_S) ≥ ρ_max for every pair inside a cluster.
   3. Does one feature per cluster guarantee VIF < 2? No. Features in different clusters can
      still be highly correlated, and pairwise bounds do not bound multi-feature VIF. Measure
       and report VIF.
   4. When does a sum of SHAP values over a coalition equal the Owen coalition value?
       When the model is additive across coalitions, such as an EBM with interactions = 0.
       Otherwise it is an approximation.
   5. Why put the absolute value outside the coalition sum, and what is the risk? It nets
       out compensating twin attributions (5.0 and −4.8 give 0.2, not 9.8). The risk is hiding
       genuinely opposing forces, so also report the sum of absolute values.
   6. Why does single-feature permutation importance mislead for twins? Shuffling one
       twin leaves the other intact, so the model barely changes. Shuffle the whole coalition
       together.
   7. Why would the truncated RBO of two identical 5-item lists be 0.556? It equals 1 − p^5
       with p = 0.85, because the tail beyond depth 5 is dropped. RBO_ext adds the
       extrapolation term and returns 1.
   8. Why can a monotone model show a negative attribution for an above-median value?
       Default attributions are measured against the mean contribution over a background,
       not against the median. A median-reference baseline makes every attribution sign
       follow the monotone direction.
   9. What does Proposition 2′ say, and what must you measure to use it? If the model shift
       is at most δ near the counterfactual and the counterfactual clears τ − δ, it survives.
       Measure the distribution of shifts between consecutive retrained models.
 10. Why is a bootstrap-dispersion margin not a conformal guarantee? Conformal
     coverage needs the test point to be exchangeable with the calibration data. Future
       retrained models under drift are not, and bootstrap spread is not the same as a
       retraining shift.
 11. Which of the three datasets has real temporal structure? Only BAF (and it is
       generated data). HELOC and Taiwan need simulated retraining and must be labelled
       that way.
 12. How many paired windows does a two-sided Wilcoxon test need to reach p < 0.05?
       At least 6 (smallest possible p is 2 / 2^6 = 0.031 at N = 6, and 0.0625 at N = 5).
